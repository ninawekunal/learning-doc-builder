## The big picture

> [!TERMS]
> - **Active recall (retrieval practice)** - pulling an answer out of your own head, instead of reading it again.
> - **Interleaving** - mixing different kinds of problems in one session instead of doing one kind at a time.
> - **Blocking** - doing one kind of problem over and over before moving to the next kind.
> - **Self-explanation** - asking yourself why something is true and how it connects to what you already know.
> - **Desirable difficulty** - a kind of effort that feels harder while practising but makes learning stick.
> - **Learning styles** - the idea that each person learns best through one sense, such as "visual learner".

[Part 1](#/docs/active-recall) showed that remembering beats rereading, that spacing helps, and that easy studying is a warning sign.

> [!TLDR]
> Two more habits make learning stick: mixing problem types, and explaining ideas to yourself.
> Both feel slower than the easy alternative, and both work better.

Table: each row is a habit from this part, what it trains, and what it costs while practising.

| Habit | What it trains | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interleaving | Choosing the right method | A lower practice score |
| Self-explanation | Seeing causes and gaps | Slower reading |
| Self-testing with a good quiz | Recall, with feedback | Getting things wrong in front of yourself |

> [!ANALOGY]
> Blocked practice is like a driving lesson on one straight road.
> You get smooth at steering, but you never practise deciding where to turn.
> Interleaving is driving through a real town, where the hard part is choosing.

> [!RECAP]
> - Mixing and explaining both feel slower, and both last longer.
> - They are two more desirable difficulties on top of recall and spacing.

## Mix up the problem types

> [!TLDR]
> Mixing different kinds of problems in one session beats doing one kind until it feels easy, because real life never tells you which method to use.

Doing ten of the same problem teaches you to *carry out* a method.
Mixing them teaches you to *choose* the method - and choosing is usually what goes wrong for real.
The catch: your score while practising will be worse, which is why almost nobody sticks with it.

> [!STEPS]
> 1. **Group things you need to tell apart**, not things that are alike.
> 2. **Shuffle them** randomly, not in a pattern you can predict.
> 3. **Ignore the practice score.** It will drop. That is expected.

> [!INTERVIEW]-
> - *Why is interleaving a desirable difficulty?* You first have to work out *which* method to use, which is the skill a real problem demands.

> [!RECAP]
> - Mix problem types so you practise choosing the method.
> - Your practice score will drop - that is expected.

## Explain it to yourself

> [!TLDR]
> Asking "why is this true?" and "how does this connect to what I already know?" while studying beats passively reading the same material.

The cheap version is to ask "why?" after every claim.
The stronger version is to teach it out loud, even to nobody.
Both force you to find the gaps that reading glides straight past.

> [!NUANCE]-
> - This works best for ideas with causes and effects. For arbitrary pairs - vocabulary, keyboard shortcuts - plain self-testing works better.
> - Explaining to a real person adds a little pressure that keeps you honest about the bits you skipped.

> [!RECAP]
> - Ask "why is this true?" after every claim.
> - Teaching it out loud exposes the gaps.
> - For arbitrary facts, self-testing beats explaining.

## How this doc format uses all of this

> [!TLDR]
> The layout is not decoration. Each part of it maps to one of the habits in this series.

Table: each row is a feature of this doc format and the study habit it relies on.

| Feature | Which habit it uses |
| --- | --- |
| Short boxes per section | Small chunks you can recall one at a time |
| "Interview must-know" bullets | Ready-made questions to test yourself with |
| Scenario quiz at the end | Remembering, with feedback |
| "Select all that apply" questions | Makes you tell similar things apart |
| An explanation after every answer | Feedback, which is what makes a wrong guess useful |
| "Copy my result" | A dated score to decide what to revisit, and when |

> [!WIN]-
> The target: someone who skimmed scores around 60% on the quiz, and someone who understood scores above 90%.
> If everyone scores high, the questions are only testing recognition.

> [!RECAP]
> - Every part of this doc format maps to a study habit.
> - A good quiz splits skimmers (~60%) from understanders (90%+).

## The honest limits

> [!TLDR]
> This research is strong, but it has edges, and one popular idea has no support at all.

> [!NUANCE]
> - Most of this research used university students learning word-based material in a lab. Real-world effects are smaller and messier.
> - Self-testing does not create understanding you never had. It protects and strengthens what you built; it cannot replace building it.
> - Learning styles (visual learner, auditory learner) have failed every controlled test of their main claim. Do not plan your studying around them.

> [!RECAP]
> - Lab results are cleaner than real life.
> - Self-testing strengthens understanding you built; it cannot build it for you.
> - Preferring a style is real; matching teaching to it does not help.

## Summary

> [!SUMMARY]
> - Mix problem types so you practise choosing the method, and expect a lower practice score.
> - Ask "why?" and explain ideas out loud, except for arbitrary facts, where self-testing wins.
> - A good quiz splits skimmers from understanders; a quiz everyone aces tests recognition.
> - The research has limits, and learning styles is not part of it.
